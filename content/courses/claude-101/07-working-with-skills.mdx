# Working with skills

A **skill** is a small, named package of know-how that Claude can pick up when it's relevant — and ignore when it isn't. Think of it as a folder that says *"if the task is X, here's how to do it well, and here are the helper scripts and templates you'll need."*

You don't *call* a skill. Claude reads the task, decides which skills apply, and uses them.

## Why skills matter

Skills do three things at once:

- **Consistency.** Every output of a kind looks the same way. Your brand stays your brand.
- **Speed.** Claude doesn't have to relearn your conventions each time.
- **Scoping.** A skill is loaded only when the task needs it. Your conversation stays small.

You'd notice a missing skill the way you'd notice a missing checklist — the work would still get done, just rougher and more inconsistent.

## What goes inside a skill

A skill is, at its smallest:

- **A short instruction file.** *"When the user asks for X, do it this way. Avoid Y. Always include Z."*
- **Optional supporting files.** Templates, helper scripts, examples, a brand color palette, a sample document to mimic.

It's the same shape as a recipe card. The name on the front, the steps inside, ingredients in the corner.

## Skills vs. Projects

These two are easy to confuse. A quick mental model:

- A **Project** sets *what* Claude is working on (the topic, the files, the audience).
- A **skill** sets *how* Claude should do a particular kind of task (the technique, the format, the conventions).

You can mix them. A Project about "Q3 board prep" can use a `slides-formatting` skill and a `financial-formatting` skill, both of which work just as well in unrelated Projects.
